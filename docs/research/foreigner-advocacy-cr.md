# A foreign resident advocating public-policy reform in Costa Rica

**Research boundary: unbounded.** Primary legal texts were downloaded and grepped locally; every quotation below
was read from the source document, not from a search summary. Where only a secondary source exists, it is named.
Where nothing authoritative exists, it says so.

## 1. Constitución Política art. 19 — verbatim text and consequences

### The text

Article 19 is the entire content of **Título III, "Los Extranjeros", Capítulo Único**:

> **Artículo 19.-** Los extranjeros tienen los mismos deberes y derechos individuales y sociales que los
> costarricenses, con las excepciones y limitaciones que esta Constitución y las leyes establecen.
>
> No pueden intervenir en los asuntos políticos del país, y están sometidos a la jurisdicción de los tribunales
> de justicia y de las autoridades de la República, sin que puedan ocurrir a la vía diplomática, salvo lo que
> dispongan los convenios internacionales.

**Confirmed:** the phrase *"No pueden intervenir en los asuntos políticos del país"* is in art. 19 itself — it opens
the second paragraph. It is not located elsewhere. The commonly cited paraphrase is accurate.

Note the structure, which matters for everything below: paragraph 1 is a **general grant of equality**; the
political-intervention clause is a **carve-out from it**. Art. 19 is not a stand-alone prohibition floating free of
the rights catalogue — it is an exception to a rule of equality, and exceptions to rights are read restrictively.

### The legal consequence of breaching it

**The user's hypothesis about Ley 8764 art. 7 is incorrect.** Art. 7 of Ley 8764 is titled nothing about political
activity — it sets out the objectives of *migration policy* (complementarity of national and migrant labour, respect
for human rights, integration, social-security enrolment). It has no bearing on political conduct.

More importantly: **Ley 8764 contains no provision that names political activity as a ground for any sanction.**
A case-insensitive grep of the full 4,700-line text for `polít` returns hits only in the phrase *"Constitución
Política"*, in the migration-policy chapter, and in the refugee chapter (persecution for *"opiniones políticas"*,
*"delitos políticos"*). There is no migration offence of "intervening in political affairs" and no ground that
mentions politics.

**But "no ground mentions politics" is not the same as "no ground could reach political conduct."** Two open-textured
public-order provisions could, and they sit at different levels of authority. The consequences are found in four
places:

| Route | Instrument | Decided by | What it reaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Criminal / electoral** | Código Electoral arts. 128, 274(a), 275(c) | Courts | Party financing only (see §2) |
| **Immigration — deportation** | Ley 8764 art. 183 | DGME | **Not applicable to a legal resident** |
| **Immigration — cancellation** | Ley 8764 art. 129(9) | **DGME (Dirección General)** | Public-order threat — **lower threshold, lower authority** |
| **Immigration — expulsion** | Ley 8764 art. 186 | Ministerio de Gobernación y Policía | Public-order threat — higher authority, graver consequences |

**Deportation (art. 183) is irrelevant to a lawful resident.** Its four grounds are purely status-based:

> **ARTÍCULO 183.-** Entiéndese por deportación, el acto ordenado por la Dirección General para poner, fuera del
> territorio nacional, a la persona extranjera que se encuentre en cualquiera de las siguientes situaciones:
> 1) Cuando haya ingresado clandestinamente al país o sin cumplir las normas que reglamenten su ingreso o
> permanencia. 2) Cuando haya obtenido el ingreso al país o su permanencia en él, por medio de declaraciones o la
> presentación de visas o documentos que hayan sido declarados falsos o alterados. 3) Cuando permanezca en el país,
> una vez vencido el plazo autorizado. 4) Cuando haya sido conminada a abandonar el país y no lo haga en el plazo
> dispuesto por la Dirección General.

Nothing about conduct, speech or politics. A resident in good standing cannot be deported under art. 183.

**Cancellation of residency (art. 129) is the route most commentary misses — and it is the more likely one.**
Título VII, Capítulo VII, Sección I of Ley 8764 empowers the *Dirección General* itself — not a Ministry — to
cancel residency, in mandatory terms (*"cancelará"*). Two of its eleven grounds matter here:

> **ARTÍCULO 129.-** La Dirección General **cancelará** la autorización de permanencia y residencia de las personas
> extranjeras, cuando: [...]
> **8) Realicen labores remuneradas sin estar autorizadas para ello.**
> [...]
> **9) Las personas cuyos antecedentes o actuaciones constituyan una amenaza en materia de seguridad y orden
> públicos.**

**Inciso 9 is a genuine public-order ground for losing residency, exercisable by the immigration authority alone.**
It is a lower-threshold, lower-authority route than ministerial expulsion: it needs only that a person's
*antecedentes o actuaciones* constitute a *threat* regarding security and public order — not that their activities
actually *compromise* peace and tranquillity, as art. 186 requires.

Consequences (arts. 130–131): cancellation means loss of legal migratory status and of the validity of the documents
evidencing it, and the person *"será conminada a abandonar el territorio nacional"* — which, if not complied with,
feeds back into deportation ground art. 183(4).

**Inciso 8 — working without authorisation — is, realistically, the highest-probability risk in this entire
document, and it has nothing to do with politics.** See §5a.

**Expulsion (art. 186) is the graver but less likely route.** This is the classic "orden público" clause, aimed
specifically at foreigners who *do* hold legal status:

> **ARTÍCULO 186.-** La expulsión es la orden emanada del Ministerio de Gobernación y Policía, en resolución
> razonada, por medio de la cual la persona extranjera **que goce de permanencia legal, bajo cualquier categoría
> migratoria**, deberá abandonar el territorio nacional, en el plazo fijado para tal efecto, cuando se considere
> que **sus actividades comprometen la paz, la seguridad pública, la tranquilidad o el orden público**.

Consequences are severe and quasi-automatic:

> **ARTÍCULO 187.-** La persona extranjera expulsada no podrá reingresar al país por el término de diez años,
> excepto si el presidente de la República lo autoriza expresamente.

> **ARTÍCULO 188.-** La resolución que ordene la expulsión de una persona extranjera implicará la pérdida de su
> condición migratoria legal, sin que ello conlleve la necesidad de realizar un procedimiento administrativo
> adicional de cancelación.

Three features of art. 186 define the risk profile honestly:

1. **It is discretionary and vague.** "Tranquilidad" and "orden público" are open-textured, and the assessment is
   *"cuando se considere"* — an administrative judgement, not a judicial finding.
2. **It is political-branch, not judicial** — the order comes from the *Ministerio de Gobernación y Policía*. But
   the procedure is real and protective (Ley 8764, Título XI):

   > **ARTÍCULO 215.-** ...el área legal de la Dirección General... conferirá a la persona extranjera un plazo de
   > tres días hábiles para que ofrezca pruebas de descargo...
   >
   > **ARTÍCULO 216.-** La persona extranjera cuya expulsión se haya ordenado, podrá interponer, en el acto de
   > notificación o dentro de los tres días hábiles siguientes... recurso de revocatoria ante la Dirección General o
   > de apelación ante el Tribunal Administrativo Migratorio. ...**La impugnación de la orden de expulsión suspenderá
   > la ejecución de esta.**

   So: a right to offer evidence, and **an appeal that automatically suspends execution** (art. 216) pending the
   Tribunal Administrativo Migratorio's decision (art. 217). A *recurso de amparo* to the Sala Constitucional on
   arts. 28/29 grounds would sit on top of that. The three-day windows are short — the practical lesson is to have a
   lawyer identified *in advance*, not to find one afterwards.
3. **It does not mention politics.** Art. 186 is a public-order clause, not an art. 19 enforcement clause. To reach
   advocacy, an authority would have to argue that the advocacy *itself* compromises public tranquillity — a
   substantial argumentative leap that, as §4 shows, no reported modern case has made.

**Bottom line for §1:** breaching art. 19 carries no automatic sanction — no fine, and no criminal offence outside
party financing. But it would be wrong to conclude there is no residency-cancellation ground — a claim commonly made
and repeated in expat guidance: **art. 129(9) exists, and is a public-order cancellation power held by DGME itself.**
Together with ministerial
expulsion under art. 186, that gives two discretionary routes — **both low probability for the activity
contemplated here, both high consequence, and neither requiring a court.**

## 2. What counts as "intervenir en los asuntos políticos"

This is the genuinely unsettled question, and the honest answer is: **the boundary is not authoritatively fixed, and
the leading definition contains a limb that literally reaches policy advocacy.** Below is the evidence in both
directions, then a judgement.

### 2a. The textual argument — that "político" means electoral

The Constitution supplies its own vocabulary, and it consistently ties "political" to **citizenship**:

> **Artículo 90.-** La ciudadanía es el conjunto de **derechos y deberes políticos** que corresponde a los
> costarricenses mayores de dieciocho años.

> **Artículo 93.-** El sufragio es función cívica primordial y obligatoria y se ejerce ante las Juntas Electorales
> en votación directa y secreta, por los **ciudadanos** inscritos en el Registro Civil.

> **Artículo 98.-** Los **ciudadanos** tendrán el derecho de agruparse en partidos para **intervenir en la política
> nacional**...

Art. 98 is the strongest textual signal available. It uses **the same verb as art. 19** — *intervenir* — and applies
it to **party activity**, reserved to *ciudadanos*. Read together, arts. 19/90/93/98 describe one closed system:
political rights are the content of citizenship; citizenship is Costa Rican; "intervening" is what one does through
parties and the ballot; foreigners are outside that system.

**The counter-signal within the same text — art. 26.** The Constitution expressly grants *everyone* the right to
gather **for political discussion**:

> **Artículo 26.-** **Todos** tienen derecho de reunirse pacíficamente y sin armas, ya sea para negocios privados, o
> para **discutir asuntos políticos y examinar la conducta pública de los funcionarios**.

This is decisive against the maximalist reading. Art. 26 uses the *identical phrase* "asuntos políticos" as art. 19,
and grants it to "Todos". If art. 19 barred foreigners from all engagement with "asuntos políticos", art. 26 would
contradict it. The only coherent reconciliation is that art. 19's *intervenir* means something narrower than
*discutir* and *examinar la conducta pública de los funcionarios* — i.e. it bars **exercising political power**, not
**discussing or scrutinising it**.

**A second structural signal — art. 60.** Where the Constitution wanted to bar foreigners from a specific
*non-electoral* leadership role, it said so expressly and separately:

> **Artículo 60.-** ...Queda prohibido a los extranjeros ejercer **dirección o autoridad en los sindicatos**.

The existence of art. 60 implies art. 19 was not already doing that work. A general gag on foreign civic
participation would have made art. 60 redundant.

### 2b. What authorities actually say

**TSE, resolución n.º 183-E-2001** (12 January 2001), **considerando II** — the narrowing rule. The TSE held that
the prohibited matters must be identifiable in the legal order, not invented ad hoc:

> Los "asuntos políticos del país", en los cuales no pueden intervenir los foráneos, deben estar previstos, al menos
> por exclusión, en la propia Constitución o en la ley, porque no otra regla puede derivarse del texto del artículo
> 19 constitucional al señalar, luego de reconocer aquellos derechos en forma general, "con las excepciones y
> limitaciones que esta Constitución y las leyes establecen".

But it added a widening caveat — connected acts count even if unlisted:

> ...es jurídicamente posible, incluir dentro del impedimento, los actos conexos o derivados de las limitaciones
> constitucionales o legales, aunque no estén señalados expresamente o por exclusión, siempre que razonablemente se
> puedan derivar de aquellas.

**The examples the TSE gave of prohibited matters were: exercising suffrage, and making financial contributions to
political parties and electoral processes.** Both electoral. That is the best available indication of how broadly the
"actos conexos" caveat was meant to reach.

**Sala Constitucional, resolución n.º 3076-2011** (9 March 2011, expediente 10-004048-0007-CO), **considerando IV** —
the leading modern statement. The case was an *acción de inconstitucionalidad* against a University of Costa Rica
rule barring foreign students from holding student-representation posts. The Sala held the prohibition limits only:

> ...la actividad de los ciudadanos, dirigida a intervenir en la designación de sus gobernantes o a influir en la
> formación de la política estatal.

and distinguished:

> ...la Sala realizó una distinción entre "participación institucional" y "participación no institucional", donde la
> primera es la que se canaliza a través de mecanismos gubernamentales de toma de decisiones, sujetas al principio de
> legalidad, y la segunda se refiere a las actividades que, lejos de formar parte de la institucionalidad, se
> orientan a ejercer presión sobre ella.

**The outcome is more favourable than the framing alone suggests:**

> En ese momento, la Sala **no encontró razón alguna para limitar la participación institucional a las personas
> extranjeras, y resolvió declarar con lugar la acción.**

That is significant. The Sala struck down the rule and found no reason to bar foreigners even from **institutional**
participation in that setting. If institutional participation was not restricted, external, non-institutional
advocacy is *a fortiori* further from the prohibition.

**The countervailing point must still be stated.** The second limb — **"influir en la formación de la política
estatal"** — describes, almost exactly, what advocating a *Ley de Eficiencia Digital* is. Read in isolation, it
reaches policy advocacy. The TSE journal's own author draws precisely this worry:

> Por ejemplo, si un grupo de personas migrantes impulsa reformas para mejorar sus condiciones en el país, se podría
> alegar que está interviniendo en política nacional con el fin de sancionarlo.

That is doctrinal commentary, not authority — but it is the clearest statement available that **promoting reforms is
the activity around which the ambiguity actually bites**, and it should not be waved away.

*Source note:* both resolutions are quoted from the TSE's own *Revista de Derecho Electoral* (Blanco Gamboa, n.º 40),
retrieved and read in the original Spanish rather than in summary. That article gives the official citations —
TSE 183-E-2001 at `tse.go.cr/juris/electorales/0183-E-2001.HTM`, and Sala Constitucional res. 3076, expediente
10-004048-0007-CO, at Nexus PJ. **I did not open the resolutions themselves**, so the quotations are the journal's
reproduction, not the primary text. They should be checked against the originals before being quoted publicly.

**Sala Constitucional, voto n.° 01691-2019** (30 January 2019, 12:30) — the one place art. 19 is applied concretely,
and it is **electoral**. Reviewing a challenge to the Código Electoral's ban on foreign party donations, the Sala
carved foreigners out of its otherwise protective holding:

> Los postulados expuestos son aplicables únicamente a las situaciones relacionadas con personas jurídicas
> nacionales, no así con personas extranjeras físicas o jurídicas, puesto que en este último caso, la propia
> Constitución Política, en su ordinal 19, prohíbe la intervención de extranjeros en los asuntos políticos del país.

*Source:* quoted verbatim from the annotation to art. 275 in the **TSE's official Código Electoral PDF** — i.e. the
electoral authority's own published text, a reliable mirror, though not the Sala's original.

**PGR dictámenes:** searched (`site:pgr.go.cr`, plus general queries). **No dictamen on the scope of art. 19's
political prohibition was found.** Stated as a negative result, not as absence of such a document.

### 2c. Where art. 19 is actually enforced: party financing

The only concrete statutory enforcement of art. 19 against a private foreign individual is the electoral-finance ban:

> **Código Electoral ARTÍCULO 128.- Prohibición de la contribución de extranjeros y personas jurídicas**
> Prohíbese a los extranjeros y a las personas jurídicas de cualquier naturaleza y nacionalidad efectuar, directa,
> indirectamente o en forma encubierta, contribuciones, donaciones o aportes, en dinero o en especie, para sufragar
> los gastos de los partidos políticos. A los extranjeros, sean personas físicas o jurídicas, también les está
> prohibido otorgar préstamos, adquirir títulos o realizar cualquier otra operación que implique beneficio de
> cualquier clase para los partidos políticos.

This carries **criminal** penalties (arts. 274(a), 275(c)), and it is broad: *"cualquier otra operación que implique
beneficio de cualquier clase para los partidos políticos"*. Party membership is likewise closed — Código Electoral
art. 49 defines parties as *"asociaciones voluntarias de ciudadanos"*.

### 2d. Answers to the six specific activities

Confidence is my assessment of the legal position, given everything above.

| | Activity | Position | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a)** | **Publish a website advocating a law** | **Permitted.** Art. 29 grants publication without prior censorship to "Todos"; art. 28 bars persecution for opinion. Non-institutional in 3076-2011 terms. Residual exposure: the "influir en la formación de la política estatal" limb. | High |
| **(b)** | **Meet legislators/ministries to propose a reform** | **Permitted as petition.** Art. 27 guarantees petition "ante cualquier funcionario público" to everyone. Proposing is not deciding. Care: this edges toward the institutional side — be a petitioner, not a participant in the deliberation. | Medium-high |
| **(c)** | **Appear at a legislative committee hearing** | **Permitted if invited as a technical witness.** Testifying on request is answering the institution, not occupying it. Costa Rican committees routinely hear foreign experts, IFI staff and foreign chamber representatives. Riskier if self-styled as a promoter of the bill rather than a technical resource. **Not verified:** I found no rule of the Asamblea's *Reglamento* addressing the nationality of *comparecientes*. | Medium |
| **(d)** | **Speak to the press about public policy** | **Permitted.** Arts. 28 and 29. This is core protected expression and clearly non-institutional. Avoid commentary on parties, candidates or elections. | High |
| **(e)** | **Form or join a civil association doing policy work** | **Join/form: permitted. But Ley 218 art. 3 is a real constraint** — see below. | Medium |
| **(f)** | **Sign or promote a petition** | **Permitted** under art. 27, which expressly covers petition *"en forma individual o colectiva"*. **Exception:** signature collection for an electoral or referendum process is electoral and closed. | High |

On **(e)**, the association point deserves precision, because two provisions of **Ley 218 de Asociaciones** are
usually overlooked:

> **Artículo 3º.-** Dentro de la autorización de esta ley **no se admitirán asociaciones de carácter político**, ni
> las que tengan por objeto un fin que fuere física o legalmente imposible...

> **Artículo 23.-** ...Quedan prohibidas en el referido local las reuniones, conferencias y toda clase de
> **manifestaciones de carácter político partidista**, así como facilitar el recinto para esa clase de actos.

> **Artículo 4º.-** El control administrativo de las asociaciones corresponde al Poder Ejecutivo, quien es el
> encargado de... **disolver las que persigan fines ilícitos o lesionen la moral o el orden público**...

Art. 3 says *"carácter político"*; art. 23 says *"político partidista"*. The narrower art. 23 wording, plus art. 6
(*"La presente ley no se aplica a los partidos políticos"*), plus the observable reality that Costa Rica has a large
registered-association advocacy sector (transparency, environmental, digital-rights NGOs), all indicate that art. 3
in practice means **partisan**, not "concerned with public policy". But the text says *político*, and art. 4 gives the
Executive a dissolution power on public-order grounds. **A policy-advocacy association is normal and low-risk in
practice; it should be drafted with statutory purposes framed as technical, educational and civic, not political.**

Two further verified points on associations: **Ley 218 contains no nationality requirement** for members or for
board members — the word *costarricense* does not appear in it, and *extranjero* appears only in relation to
foreign-*domiciled* associations (art. 16). And art. 25 of the Constitution grants association to *habitantes*:

> **Artículo 25.-** **Los habitantes** de la República tienen derecho de asociarse para fines lícitos.

### 2e. Judgement

The prohibition is **centred on electoral and institutional activity** — voting, candidacy, party membership, party
financing, holding decision-making office. That is where every concrete enforcement rule and every applied holding
sits. Policy advocacy from outside the state is, on the best reading of arts. 25–29 plus 3076-2011's
institutional/non-institutional distinction, **outside the prohibition**.

But this must be stated with its limit: **no authority squarely holds that a foreigner may lawfully campaign for
legislation**, and 3076-2011's "influir en la formación de la política estatal" limb is a real textual hook for a
hostile reading. The position is *well-supported* — it is not *settled*.

## 3. The right of petition, expression and publication — do they cover foreigners?

**Yes.** These articles are in Título IV (*Derechos y Garantías Individuales*) and are drafted **impersonally or with
universal subjects** — none is limited to *ciudadanos*. This contrast is deliberate and consistent: the Constitution
uses *ciudadanos* precisely when it means citizens (arts. 90, 93, 98) and *todos* / *habitantes* / *nadie* when it
does not.

> **Artículo 27.-** Se garantiza la libertad de petición, en forma individual o colectiva, ante cualquier
> funcionario público o entidad oficial, y el derecho a obtener pronta resolución.

**Subject: "Se garantiza" — impersonal, no subject limitation.** Petition belongs to everyone, individually or
collectively, before *any* public official or official entity, and carries a correlative right to a prompt
answer. This is the constitutional basis for writing to a diputado or a ministry.

> **Artículo 28.-** Nadie puede ser inquietado ni perseguido por la manifestación de sus opiniones ni por acto alguno
> que no infrinja la ley.
>
> Las acciones privadas que no dañen la moral o el orden públicos, o que no perjudiquen a tercero, están fuera de la
> acción de la ley.
>
> No se podrá, sin embargo, hacer en forma alguna propaganda política por clérigos o seglares invocando motivos de
> religión o valiéndose, como medio, de creencias religiosas.

**Subject: "Nadie" — everyone.** Note the third paragraph: the only speech restriction art. 28 imposes is on
**religiously-framed political propaganda**, which is irrelevant here — and its existence shows the framers legislated
speech limits explicitly when they wanted them.

> **Artículo 29.-** Todos pueden comunicar sus pensamientos de palabra o por escrito, y publicarlos sin previa
> censura; pero serán responsables de los abusos que cometan en el ejercicio de este derecho, en los casos y del modo
> que la ley establezca.

**Subject: "Todos" — everyone.** No prior censorship; liability only *ex post* and only as a law defines. A website
advocating a reform is squarely within art. 29.

Also relevant, both quoted in §2: **art. 25** (association — *habitantes*) and **art. 26** (assembly to discuss
political matters and scrutinise officials' conduct — *Todos*).

**Reinforcement from art. 19 itself:** paragraph 1 grants foreigners *"los mismos deberes y derechos individuales y
sociales que los costarricenses"*. Arts. 27–29 sit in the chapter of *derechos individuales*. So art. 19 paragraph 1
independently extends them to foreigners; paragraph 2 subtracts only *political* participation.

**Treaty layer (not independently verified here).** Costa Rica is party to the American Convention on Human Rights —
whose seat, the Inter-American Court, is in San José — and to the ICCPR. Both guarantee expression to "every person"
and permit restriction of *political rights* specifically (ACHR art. 23 is limited to *ciudadanos*, while art. 13
expression is not). This strengthens the reading that the art. 19 carve-out tracks art. 23-type political rights and
leaves art. 13-type expression intact. **I did not verify the treaty texts or any IACtHR holding in this research** —
flagged as a line of argument, not a finding.

## 4. Immigration risk under Ley 8764 — and whether anyone has actually been sanctioned

### The legal exposure

Fully set out in §1. To restate the shape of it precisely:

- **No provision of Ley 8764 names political activity as a ground for losing residency.** Verified by exhaustive
  case-insensitive grep. But **two open-textured public-order grounds could be stretched to reach it**, and it would
  be wrong to say the law offers no hook at all:
  - **Art. 129(9) — cancellation by DGME** where a person's *"antecedentes o actuaciones constituyan una amenaza en
    materia de seguridad y orden públicos"*. Lower threshold ("threat"), lower authority (the Directorate itself).
  - **Art. 186 — expulsion by the Ministry** where *"sus actividades comprometen la paz, la seguridad pública, la
    tranquilidad o el orden público"*. Higher threshold ("compromise"), higher authority, worse consequences:
    loss of status without further procedure (art. 188) and a **ten-year re-entry ban** (art. 187).
- **Deportation (art. 183) cannot reach a lawful resident** — its grounds are entry/status defects only.

So: **the risk is not that advocacy is illegal. The risk is that two broad public-order powers exist which are
capacious enough to be misused, against which the practical protection is procedural, political and reputational as
much as substantive.**

Procedural protections are real. Art. 186 requires a *resolución razonada*; art. 215 gives three days to offer
exculpatory evidence; **art. 216 provides that challenging an expulsion order suspends its execution**; art. 217
routes the appeal to the Tribunal Administrativo Migratorio; and a *recurso de amparo* to the Sala Constitucional
would be available on arts. 28/29 grounds. An expulsion or cancellation aimed at a technical website advocating
administrative efficiency would be a weak case for the State to defend and an easy one to publicise.

### Reported cases, 2015–2026

**I found no reported case, in the period 2015–2026, of a foreigner in Costa Rica being expelled, having residency
revoked, or being otherwise sanctioned for policy advocacy or political speech.** Multiple searches in Spanish and
English across news, NGO and human-rights sources returned:

- Expulsions and deportations for **serious crimes** (e.g. reporting of 28 such removals in 2026 — *Infobae*);
- Coverage of **third-country nationals expelled to Costa Rica by the US** in 2025 (HRW), which is the inverse
  situation;
- General residency-loss guidance from immigration practitioners listing status-based grounds.

None involved speech or advocacy. **Stated as a negative search result**: absence of reporting is not proof no case
exists, particularly since an administrative expulsion generates no public judgment unless someone litigates or
publicises it. But if the practice were live, one would expect NGO documentation, and none surfaced.

**Historical context, which is worth knowing precisely because it is *not* modern.** The power to expel foreigners
for political and labour activity was used systematically in Costa Rica — under **Ley n.º 13 of 1894** and its
1905/1915/1920/1936 reforms — against strike organisers, unionists and propagators of "subversive ideas"
(Llaguno, *"Peligro para la tranquilidad pública: expulsión de extranjeros y conflictividad laboral en Costa Rica,
1894–1936"*, *Diálogos Revista Electrónica de Historia*, 2020). That study does not extend past 1936, and I found no
evidence of continuity into the modern constitutional era. The lineage matters only as a reminder that art. 186 is
the descendant of a genuinely repressive instrument — not as evidence about present-day practice.

### Risk assessment

**Probability: low.** No provision targets political activity; the two public-order clauses would each need a
strained reading to reach published technical advocacy; no reported modern precedent; strong constitutional cover
under arts. 25–29; a subject matter (administrative digitalisation) that is technocratic and politically unaligned;
and an activity type (external, published advocacy) that falls on the *non-institucional* side of 3076-2011.

**Consequence: severe.** Loss of legal status, and — under expulsion — a ten-year bar on return.

**One asymmetry worth naming.** Because art. 129(9) turns on *"antecedentes o actuaciones"*, the practical risk is
not any single publication but an accumulated public profile that an official could characterise as agitation. That
is precisely what §6's framing is designed to prevent, and it is why the framing matters more than any one sentence.

**Therefore: the framing choices in §6 are worth making even though the legal risk is small.** They cost nothing and
they remove the hook.

## 5. What a foreign resident can safely do commercially

**Crucial dependency: this depends on the residency category, and the answer differs sharply between them.** Since
the author's category is unknown, the table is by category.

### 5a. Earning income — by residency category

**The governing rule for temporary residents** (Ley 8764 art. 80), which is more restrictive than commonly assumed:

> **ARTÍCULO 80.-** Los residentes temporales **únicamente podrán realizar las actividades remuneradas o lucrativas,
> por cuenta propia o en relación de dependencia, que la Dirección General les autorice.** Tal autorización tomará en
> cuenta los dictámenes de carácter recomendativo elaborados por el Ministerio de Trabajo y Seguridad Social, así
> como otros criterios de conveniencia y oportunidad.

Note it covers *por cuenta propia* — self-employment is **not** a way around it. And **the permanent-resident rule**
(Reglamento de Extranjería, Decreto 37112-GOB):

> **Artículo 74.-** El otorgamiento de la residencia permanente implicará que la persona extranjera podrá laborar en
> **cualquier actividad laboral o intelectual remunerada**.

> **Artículo 61.-** La persona extranjera que goce de la condición migratoria de residente permanente; residente
> temporal **por vínculo con cónyuge costarricense**; categorías especiales de asilado político, apátrida, de
> refugio, víctima de trata... podrá laborar en cualquier actividad laboral o intelectual remunerada.

The Reglamento defines the status these confer:

> **LIBERACIÓN DE CONDICIÓN (LIBRE DE CONDICIÓN):** Autorización dada por la Dirección General para ejercer
> **cualquier actividad laboral o intelectual remunerada**.

| Category | Legal basis | May earn income? |
|---|---|---|
| **Residente permanente** | Regl. art. 74; Ley 8764 art. 77 | **Yes — unrestricted.** Any remunerated work or intellectual activity. This is the category that cleanly permits consulting. |
| **Temporal, spouse of Costa Rican** | Regl. art. 61 | **Yes — unrestricted** (*libre de condición*). |
| **Pensionado** (temporal; ≥ US$1,000/mo foreign pension — Ley 8764 art. 81) | Ley 8764 art. 80 | **Restricted.** Only activities DGME authorises. Passive income and company ownership are fine; personally providing paid consulting requires authorisation. |
| **Rentista** (temporal; ≥ US$2,500/mo — Ley 8764 art. 82) | Ley 8764 art. 80 | **Restricted.** Same as pensionado. |
| **Inversionista** (temporal — Ley 8764 art. 79(4)) | **Reglamento art. 93** | **Prohibited outright** — see below. Stricter than art. 80. |
| **Ejecutivo / representante / gerente / personal técnico** (Ley 8764 art. 79(3), art. 83) | Ley 8764 arts. 80, 83 | **Restricted to the authorised role**, with a salary floor of minimum wage + 25% (art. 83). |
| **Profesional / científico / técnico especializado** (art. 79(5)) | Ley 8764 art. 80 | **Restricted** to the authorised activity. |

**The inversionista category is far more restrictive than commonly reported**, and this correction matters because
"inversionista" is often recommended to exactly this kind of entrepreneur:

> **Reglamento de Extranjería, Artículo 93.-** La persona extranjera a la que se le otorgue la residencia temporal
> como inversionista y sus dependientes **no podrán realizar ninguna labor manual o intelectual remunerada**.

That is an absolute bar on personally performing paid work — not a requirement to seek authorisation. An
inversionista may own the company and receive returns on the investment, but **may not bill for consulting.**

**A widely-repeated shortcut worth correcting** for rentistas and pensionados: it is often said they "can own a
company and receive dividends but cannot work". The *dividends* half is right — **receiving profits as an owner is
not an "actividad remunerada o lucrativa" performed by the person**. The *"cannot work"* half is imprecise for these
two categories: art. 80 does not prohibit work, it makes work **conditional on DGME authorisation**. The route is to
*apply*, not to assume it is closed. (For inversionistas, by contrast, Reglamento art. 93 really is a prohibition.)

> ### ⚠ The most probable risk in this document
>
> Ley 8764 **art. 129(8)** makes it a mandatory ground of residency cancellation to
> *"realicen labores remuneradas sin estar autorizadas para ello."*
>
> **A rentista, pensionado or inversionista who invoices consulting work off the back of this portfolio — the
> stated commercial goal — risks losing residency under a clear, specific, non-discretionary rule.** This is a far
> more likely path to immigration trouble than anything in §§1–4, it requires no hostile interpretation of art. 19,
> and it is entirely avoidable: resolve work authorisation (or move to permanent residency) *before* invoicing.
>
> **Not verified:** how actively DGME enforces art. 129(8); how readily art. 80 authorisations are granted; and
> whether an inversionista who manages their own company is treated as performing *labor intelectual remunerada*.
> These are the first questions for an immigration `abogado`.

**Naturalisation** is the durable answer to all of this and to §§1–4 as well: it removes art. 19 entirely. The
constitutional residence periods are **verified**:

> **Artículo 14.-** Son costarricenses por naturalización: [...] **2)** Los nacionales de otros países de
> Centroamérica, los españoles y los iberoamericanos por nacimiento que hayan residido oficialmente en el país
> durante **cinco años** como mínimo... **3)** Los centroamericanos, los españoles y los iberoamericanos que no lo
> sean por nacimiento y **los demás extranjeros** que hayan residido oficialmente en el país durante **siete años**
> como mínimo...

Art. 14(5) also provides a two-year route for the spouse of a Costa Rican. The *"demás requisitos que fije la ley"*
(language tests, civics exam, procedure) are in Ley 1155 and are **not verified here** — see the TSE's
*Naturalización por residencia* requirements sheet.

### 5b. Owning a Costa Rican company

**Ownership: yes.** The Código de Comercio imposes no nationality requirement on shareholders or *cuotistas*. Art. 120
provides that shares may be issued *"en moneda nacional o extranjera"* and must be nominative; no nationality
restriction on holders appears anywhere in the sociedades chapter. Two persons are required to incorporate an S.A.
or S.R.L., after which shares may be consolidated in one holder — **that mechanic is from practitioner sources
(AG Legal, BGA Corp, Navas), not verified against the Código text**, so treat "100% single-owner after
consolidation" as practitioner consensus rather than a quoted rule.

**But there is an on-the-books restriction almost never mentioned, and it should be flagged:**

> **Código de Comercio ARTÍCULO 8.-** No podrán ejercer el comercio, aunque tengan capacidad conforme al derecho
> común: a) Los privados de ese derecho por sentencia judicial; b) Los quebrados o insolventes no rehabilitados; y
> c) Los funcionarios públicos a quienes la ley prohíba tal ejercicio.
>
> **Los extranjeros podrán ejercer el comercio en el territorio nacional, siempre que se hayan establecido
> permanentemente en el país, con residencia no menor de 10 años**, sometidos al régimen jurídico y a la
> jurisdicción de los tribunales de la República, **salvo lo que sobre el particular consignen los tratados o
> convenios internacionales**.
>
> *(Así reformado mediante la Ley N° 4625 de 30 de julio de 1970)*

Read literally, a foreigner may "ejercer el comercio" only after **ten years'** permanent establishment. Important
qualifications, in order of weight:

1. It restricts *ejercer el comercio* — acting as a **comerciante** in the Code's own technical sense, which the
   Code defines:

   > **ARTÍCULO 5.-** Son comerciantes: **a)** Las personas con capacidad jurídica que ejerzan **en nombre propio**
   > actos de comercio, haciendo de ello su **ocupación habitual**; [...] **c)** Las sociedades que se constituyan de
   > conformidad con disposiciones de este Código, cualquiera que sea el objeto o actividad que desarrollen...

   A shareholder does not trade *en nombre propio* — under art. 5(c) **the company is itself the comerciante**. So
   owning an S.A. is not "exercising commerce"; art. 8 does not reach it. Art. 6 adds that occasional acts of
   commerce do not make one a comerciante either.
2. It is expressly **subordinated to treaties** (*"salvo lo que... consignen los tratados o convenios
   internacionales"*), and Costa Rica's trade agreements (CAFTA-DR among them) contain national-treatment
   obligations.
3. It is a 1970 text sitting uneasily with art. 19's equality paragraph and would be vulnerable to constitutional
   challenge.
4. **Not verified:** whether art. 8 has ever been enforced in the modern era. I found no case. It appears to be
   dormant, and no practitioner guide I saw mentions it.

**Practical import: incorporate and trade through a Costa Rican company rather than as an individual sole trader.**
That is the standard structure anyway, and it sidesteps art. 8 entirely.

### 5c. Registering a trademark (Ley 7978)

**Yes, with no nationality barrier.** Ley 7978 contains no requirement that an applicant be Costa Rican. The only
provision touching foreignness is a **domicile-based agent rule**:

> Cuando el solicitante o el titular de un derecho de propiedad industrial tenga su domicilio o sede **fuera de Costa
> Rica**, deberá ser representado por un mandatario con domicilio en el país.

**A resident is domiciled in Costa Rica**, so this does not bite — a foreign *resident* may file in their own name
without a local agent. (A trademark could equally be held by the Costa Rican company.) *Verified by reading the Ley
7978 text; the mirror was UAIPIT, cross-referenced against WIPO Lex's listing of the same law.*

### 5d. Contracting with the State (Ley 9986)

**Verified by exhaustive search of the full text: Ley 9986 contains no nationality restriction on bidders.** The word
*extranjero* does not appear anywhere in its 136 articles, and neither does *reciprocidad*. There is no local-content
rule and no reciprocity condition in the statute.

What the law does contain is an affirmative equality principle:

> **f) Principio de igualdad y libre concurrencia:** en los procedimientos de contratación pública se dará un trato
> igualitario a todos los oferentes, se procurará la más amplia competencia y se invitará a potenciales oferentes
> idóneos. **No se podrán establecer restricciones injustificadas a la libre participación.**

That last sentence cuts in favour of a foreign-owned bidder: excluding one without justification would itself breach
the principle.

**Two corrections and one caveat.** A widely-circulated summary attributes a "no less favourable treatment... on the
basis of mutual reciprocity and national treatment" clause to Ley 9986. **That language is not in Ley 9986** — it
belongs to the superseded Ley 7494 or to regulations. Do not cite it. **Not verified:** the *Reglamento* to Ley 9986
and the SICOP supplier-registration requirements, which is where practical obstacles (legal representation, local
tax registration, *personería jurídica*) would actually live. The realistic route is bidding **through the Costa
Rican company**, registered in SICOP and tax-registered — which makes the bidder a Costa Rican legal person and
renders the question largely moot.

## 6. Framing the site, and whether partners change the picture

### 6a. The governing principle

Every framing recommendation below follows from one distinction — the one the Sala drew in 3076-2011 and the one
arts. 26–29 embody:

> **Contributing to a public debate is protected. Seeking to exercise or allocate political power is not.**

So: **be a technical author, not a political actor.** Publish work; do not run a campaign. Offer a demonstration;
do not mobilise a constituency. Address officials as a petitioner under art. 27; do not address voters.

### 6b. Wording that keeps you clearly on the safe side

**Lead with what you actually are — and are demonstrably.** The strongest protection is that the claim is *true*:
this is a working software demonstration with a public repository.

- *"A technical demonstration of how a once-only e-government platform could work in Costa Rica."*
- *"An open-source reference implementation, published as a professional portfolio."*
- *"Built by [name], a software architect resident in Costa Rica."* — **state the foreign residency openly.**
  Transparency is protective; discovered concealment is what looks bad.
- *"The data are fictional; no real personal data are used."* — already true of the demo, and worth surfacing.
- *"This is a contribution to a technical conversation, offered for the consideration of anyone working on public-sector
  modernisation."*
- On the proposed law: **describe it as an illustrative artefact, not a demand.** *"To make the discussion concrete,
  the demo includes a sketch of what enabling legislation might contain."* / *"a worked example of the legal
  scaffolding such a system would need."*
- **Invite correction rather than support:** *"Feedback from Costa Rican jurists and public servants is welcome."*
  Soliciting expert critique is the posture of an author; soliciting signatures is the posture of a campaigner.
- A one-line disclaimer does real work: *"Independent technical work. Not affiliated with any political party,
  campaign or public institution."*

**On the name `sindarvueltas.org`.** *"Sin dar vueltas"* — "without running around [in circles]" — is a
citizen-experience complaint about bureaucratic runaround, not a political slogan. It names the *problem the software
solves*. That is fine, and better than a name evoking reform or movement. Keep the site's voice matching it:
service-design language, not political language.

### 6c. Wording that is risky — and why

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| "Campaign", "movement", "join us", "sign here" | Constructs a political mobilisation. Signature-gathering is the closest civil activity to an electoral act. |
| "Demand", "we call on the Asamblea", "the government must" | Adversarial posture toward institutions; invites the *"presión sobre la institucionalidad"* characterisation. |
| Naming, endorsing, criticising or scoring **parties, candidates, diputados or the President** | The clearest red line. Personalised political criticism by a foreigner is where art. 19 has real bite, and near an election it is worse. |
| Any election-timed push, or referencing an electoral cycle | Proximity to a campaign converts advocacy into electoral intervention in the eyes of the TSE. |
| Soliciting or making **donations** connected to anything political | Código Electoral art. 128 is criminal and extremely broad. Do not fundraise for advocacy at all. |
| "Ley de Eficiencia Digital" presented as **your bill** that you are pushing | Authoring illustrative text is fine; *promoting a legislative proposal* is the phrasing that best fits "influir en la formación de la política estatal". Frame as example, never as sponsorship. |
| Claiming institutional endorsement you don't have | Independently damaging, and forfeits the truthfulness that protects the rest. |

**One further point, orthogonal but real:** the project's existing conventions — *"Legal claims are sourced"*, the
DEMO disclaimer, fictional citizens — are themselves a risk control. A demo that visibly refuses to overstate is a
demo that is hard to characterise as propaganda. Keep the disclaimer prominent on the public site, and keep the
`docs/research/` sourcing visible, since it evidences good faith.

### 6d. Do Costa Rican partners change the picture?

**Legally: no — and this is the point most people get wrong.** Art. 19 binds the individual foreigner. Nothing in it
is cured by a co-author, a Costa Rican board, or an institutional host. If an activity were prohibited, doing it
through a partner would not launder it; if it is permitted (as §2 concludes advocacy is), no partner is needed to
make it lawful. **A Costa Rican partner does not create a legal permission that art. 19 withholds.**

**Practically: yes, substantially — on three distinct mechanisms.**

1. **The public face shifts.** With a Costa Rican co-author or a national association as publisher, the initiative
   is a Costa Rican initiative that a resident foreigner contributed to. Art. 19 is then not merely satisfied but
   never seriously raised.
2. **Institutional hosting recharacterises the activity.** A **university** (TEC, UCR, ULACIT) converts the work into
   *academic research* — the most protected category available, and one where foreign researchers are entirely
   normal. Ley 8764 art. 79(5) explicitly contemplates *científicos, profesionales, técnicos especializados*, and the
   Reglamento defines an **INVESTIGADOR** category as a foreigner invited by a public or private institution to do
   research of national interest. Academic framing also gives access to committee hearings as a technical expert
   (§2, (c)) on the safest possible footing.
3. **A chamber (CAMTIC, UCCAEP) makes it industry policy work.** Chambers lobby on public policy as a matter of
   routine and include foreign-owned member firms. Contributing through CAMTIC makes the advocacy *sectoral* rather
   than personal, and CAMTIC has standing with MICITT that an individual does not. **This is likely the highest-value
   partnership for the stated goal of winning work**, since it is simultaneously the safest framing and a
   business-development channel.

**Costs and cautions on partnering.** A **Ley 218 association** brings art. 3 (*no asociaciones de carácter
político*) and art. 4 (Executive dissolution power) into play — draft the statutory purposes as technical/educational,
and if the only aim is to publish a demo, an association may be unnecessary overhead versus simply using the company.
A university or chamber partnership means **ceding editorial control** and moving at institutional speed. And note
that a foreigner may hold association office (Ley 218 has no nationality rule) but **may not hold *dirección o
autoridad* in a trade union** (Constitution art. 60) — relevant only if a union were ever a partner.

### 6e. The one thing worth spending money on

Retain a Costa Rican `abogado` for a **short written opinion** covering, in this order of priority:

1. **The author's residency category and what it permits him to be paid for** — Ley 8764 art. 80 / Reglamento
   art. 93, and the art. 129(8) cancellation risk. This is the concrete, high-probability issue, and it needs
   answering *before* the first invoice, not before the first blog post.
2. Whether the intended advocacy falls outside art. 19 as construed in res. 3076-2011.
3. Review of the About page wording before publication.

This is a few hours of work. It converts the residual risk in §§4–5 from an open question into a documented,
professionally-advised position — which is itself the best possible answer if anyone ever asks.
